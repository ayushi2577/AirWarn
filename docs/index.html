Can we warn a day ahead when a city's air will be unhealthy?

Using past air-quality readings from 26 Indian cities (2015 to mid-2020), we predict whether tomorrow's AQI will be above 200 ("Poor" or worse), and test it honestly against the simple rule "tomorrow will be like today".

01 Question

Poor-air days harm children, older people, and people with asthma or heart disease. A one-day warning lets people stay indoors, wear masks or reschedule outdoor activity.

Question: using a city's air-quality readings up to the end of today, can we predict whether tomorrow's AQI will be above 200, better than the rule "tomorrow will be like today"?

ElementDefinition
One row (unit)One city on one day t (26 cities, 2015–2020).
Targetpoor_tomorrow = 1 if the city's AQI on day t+1 is above 200, else 0. We chose 200 because it is the official CPCB boundary for "Poor", before seeing any results, and never adjusted it to improve scores. Tomorrow's value is found by looking up the same city's row dated exactly t+1, not the next row in the table (a missing day could otherwise make "the next row" several days later).
TaskBinary classification, about 26% positive.
Prediction timeThe end of day t, once that day's readings are in. The warning is for day t+1.
Information available thenDay t's pollutants and AQI; AQI, PM2.5 and PM10 from 1, 2, 3 and 7 days earlier; the average of the last 3 and last 7 days (counted back from today, never including tomorrow); today's AQI minus yesterday's (positive means getting worse); the month, the season and the day of the week; and which city it is.
Not allowed (leakage)Anything from day t+1 or later, windows that look forward, and filling blanks using future days. An automated test checks this.
Rows usedOnly rows where both today's and tomorrow's AQI are observed (24,343 rows), the same rows for every model and baseline.
Cost of errorsA missed bad-air day costs more than a false alarm, so we aim for high recall while watching precision.

Why ML if AQI is a formula? The formula only turns today's readings into today's AQI. Tomorrow's readings do not exist yet when we predict, so the model must learn patterns from history.

What this project claims, and what it does not

We claimFor these 26 monitored cities between 2015 and mid-2020, past readings predict next-day AQI above 200 with higher precision than "tomorrow = today" at the same recall.
We do not claimThat any pollutant causes bad air; that it works for other cities, whole-city exposure or today's conditions; or that it replaces weather-based forecasts (our data has no weather).

What it is: a chemical from vehicles and industry. What we did: dropped the column entirely. It is 61% blank and never measured in 14 cities, so filling it would mostly invent values.

PM10

What it is: coarse dust particles; never measured in Lucknow. What it is: ammonia, from farming and waste; never measured in Ahmedabad. What we did: 3-day forward-fill, a "was missing" flag, training-median fill, capping. The flag lets the model tell "not measured" from "low".

27%

What it is: a chemical from vehicles and industry. What we did: 3-day forward-fill, a "was missing" flag, training-median fill, capping.

Benzene
19%

What it is: a chemical from vehicles and industry. What we did: 3-day forward-fill, a "was missing" flag, training-median fill, and capping (values reach 455, far above typical readings).

Quality issues, and how we dealt with each

Click the arrow to see what we did.

Blanks, and pollutants never measured in some cities (for example no PM10 in Lucknow)

Short gaps (up to 3 days) take the city's last known value, using only the past. Remaining blanks get the training median, with "was missing" flags on the four columns that have many gaps. Xylene is dropped.

Mumbai has AQI on only 775 of 2,009 days

Days without AQI on both days are not used, so Mumbai contributes few rows. We list this under limitations, and its results are marked unreliable where positives are few.

Uneven coverage: only 9 cities have 2015 data; 8 cities appear only in 2019–2020

City is one-hot encoded so unseen cities get all-zero columns instead of an error. Headline results use the 18 cities seen in training; the 8 unseen cities are reported in their own table as "transfer to a new city".

AQI was computed by the uploader and is not capped at 500 (maximum 2,049)

Our target only asks whether AQI is above 200, so the missing cap does not change a label. As an input, extreme values are clipped at the training 99.5th percentile. We state it as a limitation.

Suspicious values: PM10 exactly 1,000, PM2.5 up to 950, Benzene up to 455

Clipped at the 99.5th percentile of the training years only, so a few sensor-limit readings cannot dominate scaling. The cap is learned inside the pipeline and never sees validation or test rows.

No weather data (wind, rain, temperature)

We cannot fix this with this dataset. It is our main limitation and the most likely reason sudden clean-to-bad flips are mostly missed (only about 10% caught).

2020 includes the COVID lockdown (from 25 March)

2020 is never used for training or tuning. It is tested once as a stress test in two parts (pre-lockdown and lockdown), and the lockdown part is flagged unreliable because it has only 32 positives.

Rows used. We keep a row only if both today's and tomorrow's AQI are observed: 24,343 rows, 6,330 positive (26.0%). 4,656 rows lose their target because tomorrow's AQI is blank, 26 rows (each city's last day) have no next day, and 506 more are dropped because today's AQI is blank. Every model and baseline uses exactly these rows, so the persistence baseline always reads a real observation.

03 Preprocessing, and why

How to read “Fixed rule or fitted?” A fixed rule needs no numbers from the data (for example, copy yesterday’s value into a blank). A fitted step calculates a number from data (median, 99.5% cap, scaling, city list), so we calculate it from training rows only. Otherwise 2019 would influence the numbers used to predict 2019. All fitted steps sit inside a scikit-learn Pipeline, which fits them on the training rows and only applies them to validation and test rows.

04 Baselines and models

Baselines

MajorityAlways "not Poor". About 74% accuracy but zero bad days caught, which is why we do not use accuracy.
PersistenceBad tomorrow if today's AQI is above 200. Assumes air quality changes slowly from one day to the next, which makes it strong. The main bar to beat.
Tuned single featureBad tomorrow if today's AQI is above a cutoff chosen on validation (234). Assumes today's AQI alone carries the signal. Shows what the extra features add beyond it.

Models

  • Logistic regression: a weighted sum of the features turned into a probability, trained by minimizing cross-entropy loss. We use it because it gives probabilities (needed for a threshold), is easy to interpret, and fits a warning system. Assumes each feature shifts the odds of a bad day in a straight-line way. L2 penalty, C = 1.
  • Linear SVM: finds the widest-margin straight boundary using hinge loss. Expected to behave much like logistic regression. Assumes the classes can be separated by a straight boundary.
  • RBF SVM: a curved boundary through a kernel. One exact fit took 3 seconds on about 10,000 rows, so we used the exact kernel and did not need the Nystroem approximation. Platt scaling turns its scores into probabilities. Assumes a smooth, curved boundary can separate the classes.

The tuned linear SVM (C = 0.01) scored a mean PR-AUC of 0.9662. RBF SVM settings: C = 4, gamma = 0.003, after a second wider grid found nothing better. Linear SVM was slightly below RBF, so RBF was carried forward.

How we chose between models? We compared logistic regression with the best SVM on the same four validation folds, looking at the per-fold differences rather than only the average. Because fold scores vary by about 0.010, we only prefer the more complex model if it is better in most folds and clearly better on average; otherwise we prefer the simpler, easier-to-explain logistic regression. Result: the SVM’s mean PR-AUC gain (0.0018) was smaller than the fold-to-fold spread of the differences (0.0033), and at 80% recall logistic regression had higher precision in all four folds, so we chose logistic regression. The 2019 test did not influence this choice

Regularization

L1 and L2 differ by about 0.0002 in PR-AUC, far below the fold spread (about 0.010), so regularization does not change the comparison. For the SVM, C is its regularization and was tuned.

  • Split by time, never shuffled. Train 2015–2018, test 2019 (used once), 2020 as a stress test in two parts.
  • Why not random. Neighbouring days are almost the same, so a random split would let the model see the answer.
  • Embargo day. The last day before each cutoff is dropped, because its label (tomorrow's AQI) falls in the next period.
  • Validation. Four forward-chaining half-year folds inside 2015–2018, each training on all earlier dates. We built them from dates because scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit splits by row position and would mix cities.
  • Metrics. PR-AUC is the main metric because only 26% of days are positive. We also report precision, recall, F1 and ROC-AUC. Accuracy is shown only to explain why it misleads.
  • Threshold. Chosen on pooled validation as the highest precision with recall at least 80%, then frozen. It was never re-tuned on the test.
  • Change (flip) days. Days when today’s status differs from tomorrow’s, for example AQI 180 today and 230 tomorrow. Persistence is wrong on every one by construction, so they show what a model adds.
  • Seen vs new cities. Headline results use the 18 cities seen in training. The 8 never-seen cities are reported separately.
  • Uncertainty. A block bootstrap resamples whole weeks, because consecutive days are correlated.
  • Test rule. The test set was used once, after everything was frozen, and it never influenced a choice.

07 Results

Result in one line: on the 2019 test (seen cities), logistic regression reaches about 94% precision and 78% recall. At the same recall as “tomorrow = today”, it is about 6.7 points more precise. It mostly follows today’s AQI and misses most sudden clean-to-bad jumps.

Headline: 2019 test, 18 seen cities

Frozen threshold, run once, 95% block-bootstrap intervals over weeks. Use the tabs to see new cities and the 2020 periods.

How to read it.

1. At the frozen threshold, logistic regression has far higher precision than persistence (0.94 vs 0.85) but lower recall (0.78 vs 0.85). The validation target was 0.80. Recall drifted down because bad days were 33% of training but 22.6% of 2019.

2. Equal-recall check (run after seeing the test, not used to change anything): at persistence's recall of 0.849, logistic regression reached precision 0.913 vs 0.847, a gain of +6.7 points (95% interval +5.0 to +8.6). The SVM gave +6.4 (+4.6 to +8.4). The gain is real but modest.

3. On validation, the tuned single-feature rule (AQI above 234) reached precision 0.944 at recall 0.80, against 0.959 for logistic regression. Most of the signal sits in today's AQI; the extra features add about 1.6 points. We did not score this rule on the test year.

4. We chose logistic regression by a rule set before seeing results: pick the SVM only if it clearly wins in most folds. It did not (PR-AUC 0.958 vs 0.959 on the 2019 test), so we kept the simpler model.

New cities (2019, 762 rows, 114 positive). Precision stays high (0.92) but recall falls to 0.54, so transfer to a city the model has never seen is weaker.

2020. Even before lockdown, recall falls to 0.69 (precision 0.88). During lockdown there are only 22 positives in seen cities and 10 in new ones, so those numbers are too few to trust and we give no intervals.

Per-city results

Recall is high in heavily polluted cities (Ahmedabad 0.94, Delhi 0.86 in 2019) and lower where bad days are rarer or less persistent (Brajrajnagar 0.56). Cities with fewer than 30 bad days are faded and unreliable; cities with none are omitted.

Which information matters? Ablation (validation)

One feature group removed at a time, logistic regression refitted on the same folds. The dashed line marks the fold-to-fold spread of PR-AUC (about 0.010); changes smaller than that are not distinguishable from noise.

Particulates and recent AQI history matter most; gases, calendar and city add nothing measurable. This shows what the model uses, not what causes bad air. The groups overlap (AQI is built from PM2.5 and PM10), so each drop understates that group’s importance.

08 Errors

All error analysis uses validation predictions only (2017–2018). The test year was not used.

  • Of 8,736 rows, 520 were missed bad days and 90 were false alarms, about 6 misses per false alarm. This is expected at a threshold set for 80% recall.
  • When today is already bad, the model catches 88% of tomorrow's bad days. When today is clean and tomorrow is bad (a flip), it catches only 12.6% (36 of 285). On ordinary days precision is 0.995 and recall 0.884; on flip days precision is 0.313 and recall 0.126. Most of the model's success is persistence-like.
  • Missed bad days sit close to the line: average AQI today is about 206, against 391 on caught days.
  • Recall is lowest in March to May (0.69 to 0.73) and partly misses October jumps (Diwali and crop burning).
  • Rows needing imputation had higher recall (0.85) than complete rows (0.74). We read this as a city effect (persistently polluted cities have more blanks), not as imputation helping.

09 Limitations

  • No weather data. Wind and rain strongly affect pollution, so sudden flips are mostly missed.
  • Mostly follows today's AQI. The gain over persistence is real but modest, and over the single-feature rule it is small.
  • Selection bias. A row exists only when AQI was reported on both days. If sensors fail more on bad days, the days we use are not a random sample.
  • Coverage. 26 monitored cities, not all of India; stations do not represent whole cities; Mumbai's AQI is mostly blank; 8 cities never appear in training.
  • Dataset AQI. It was computed by the uploader, is uncapped and has implausible values. Our target (above 200) is not affected by the cap.
  • Threshold drift. Bad days were 33% of training and 22.6% of 2019, so recall landed at 0.78 instead of 0.80. We report the real number rather than re-tune.
  • New cities. Recall falls to 0.54 in 2019.
  • 2020 and the lockdown. Performance already falls before lockdown, and the lockdown has only 32 positives.
  • Correlated days. The effective sample size is smaller than the row count.
  • Judgement calls. Imputation and capping choices.
  • We cannot claim causation, performance in other cities or performance today, since the data ends in 2020.

10 Conclusion

We chose logistic regression, following the rule we wrote before the results: the SVM was not clearly better, and logistic regression is simpler and easier to explain.

Against persistence, it raises far fewer false alarms at the frozen threshold, and gives about 6.7 points higher precision at equal recall. Its main weakness is that it catches only about 10% of days when clean air turns bad, probably because there is no weather data. Since a missed warning costs more than a false alarm, a lower threshold (try the explorer) is the sensible way to run it. A modest, honestly measured gain over a strong baseline is the result.

11 Reproduce, code and disclosure

  1. Get the data. The raw file is not in our repository, and the scripts do not download it on their own. Download city_day.csv from the Kaggle page and put it in data/raw/. A free Kaggle account is needed; never commit kaggle.json or the raw data.
  2. Create a virtual environment and install requirements.txt.
  3. Run the scripts in the order given in the README. The split dates, embargo days and frozen settings are in the repository.
  4. Run the final test and the equal-recall check once, with outputs/results/frozen_settings.json unchanged.

Split dates: train 2015-01-01 to 2018-12-30; test 2019-01-01 to 2019-12-30; 2020 pre-lockdown to 2020-03-23; lockdown 2020-03-25 to 2020-07-01. Embargo days: 2018-12-31, 2019-12-31, 2020-03-24.
